The Hall–Mills Murders

Unsolved Case No one was ever convicted in this case. Frances Hall, Henry Stevens, and William "Willie" Stevens were tried and acquitted in December 1926; charges against a fourth suspect, Henry Carpender, were dropped before he was ever tried. The case remains officially unsolved, and this page does not resolve it — nor did the episode.

This page lists the sources behind the claims made in this episode, tiered by how well-established each one is. Confirmed facts come from court records, police reports, or multiple independent contemporaneous news sources. Disputed items have conflicting accounts. Single-source claims rest on one account with no corroboration — treat them accordingly.

Confirmed Facts

10 items
Confirmed
Rev. Edward Wheeler Hall, 41, and Eleanor Reinhardt Mills, 34, were last seen alive the evening of September 14, 1922; their bodies were found on September 16, 1922, in a field called De Russey's Lane, straddling the Franklin Township (Somerset County) and New Brunswick (Middlesex County) line in New Jersey.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; Rutgers University Libraries
Confirmed
Hall was shot once in the head. Mills was shot three times in the head and her throat was cut severely enough to nearly decapitate her.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters
Confirmed
The bodies were found laid side by side beneath a crab apple tree. Torn love letters between the two were scattered at the scene, Hall's calling card was propped at his feet, his hat covered his face, and her scarf was wrapped around her neck.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; The Vintage News
Confirmed
The crime scene was never properly secured. A jurisdictional dispute between Middlesex and Somerset County authorities stalled evidence handling while crowds of onlookers walked the field taking souvenirs, and on weekends the site reportedly drew an estimated thousand cars a day, with vendors selling food and balloons and the crab apple tree itself stripped of bark and branches by souvenir hunters.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; Harold Schechter, "The Hall-Mills Murder Trial, 1926," Yale Review; Dying Words
Confirmed
The original 1922–23 investigation, led by Middlesex County prosecutor Joseph Stricker, produced no indictments. Evidence and grand jury paperwork later went missing, and the prosecutor's own brother was separately accused of trying to sell grand jury testimony to a newspaper.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters
Confirmed
Amid tabloid pressure, New Jersey Governor A. Harry Moore ordered the case reopened in 1926. Frances Hall, Henry Stevens, and William "Willie" Stevens were tried November 3 – December 3, 1926 in Somerville, NJ; a fourth defendant, cousin Henry Carpender, won a separate trial that never took place, and charges against him were later dropped.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; Rutgers University Libraries
Confirmed
The prosecution's central eyewitness was Jane Gibson, a local farmer nicknamed "the Pig Woman" by the press because she raised hogs. She testified from a hospital bed wheeled into the courtroom, and her account — including hearing a woman scream "Don't!" and a name, "Henry," shouted — had shifted across her earlier statements to police and the press.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters
Confirmed
The trial drew roughly 300 reporters, including Damon Runyon and H.L. Mencken — press coverage historians describe as unmatched in scale until the Lindbergh kidnapping trial nearly a decade later.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; Rutgers University Libraries
Confirmed
Willie Stevens's fingerprint was identified on Hall's calling card found at the scene. He also owned a .32-caliber pistol — matching the murder weapon's caliber — with its firing mechanism filed down. Henry Stevens presented an alibi (fishing, miles away) corroborated by three witnesses.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters
Confirmed
All three defendants were acquitted on December 3, 1926. The case has never been reopened and remains officially unsolved; every named principal in the case was dead by 1992, and no DNA testing or forensic re-examination has been reported.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters

Disputed / Theories

2 items
Disputed
Whether Frances Hall and her brothers were actually guilty remains disputed among historians who have examined the same circumstantial case. Gerald Tomlinson (1999) concluded the Stevens siblings were guilty; William Kunstler later concluded the jury's acquittal was probably the correct outcome given the evidence actually presented at trial.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; Rutgers University Libraries
Disputed
Reports from the case's early days described fresh scratches on Frances Hall's cheek, cited by some as part of the circumstantial case against her — but this detail was never independently corroborated to the same standard as the fingerprint or the pistol evidence presented at trial.
Source: Wikipedia — Hall–Mills murder case; EBSCO Research Starters

Single-Source Claims

4 items
Single source
St. John's vestryman Ralph Gorsline was in De Russey's Lane himself on the night of the murders, with a woman who was not his wife. He denied being there in 1922, then admitted at the 1926 trial that he had been present and had heard shots around 10:15 p.m.
Source: Crime Library — The Hall-Mills Murders
Single source
Jane Gibson's account reportedly grew more detailed over the four years between the murders and the 1926 trial, with new details increasingly resembling the version of events already circulating in tabloid coverage and the prosecution's theory.
Source: Weird NJ — Hall-Mills Murders
Single source
Gibson said she was riding her mule — named Jenny in some accounts — to check her property for suspected thieves on the night she claimed to witness the killing.
Source: Weird NJ — Hall-Mills Murders
Single source
The New York Times alone stationed four full-time stenographers at the trial to transcribe testimony, and newspapers wired an estimated twelve million words out of the courthouse by the time the trial concluded.
Source: Harold Schechter, "The Hall-Mills Murder Trial, 1926," Yale Review
