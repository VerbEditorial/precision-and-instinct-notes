The Mad Butcher of Kingsbury Run

Open Case This case remains officially unsolved, and identification efforts are ongoing: the Cuyahoga County Medical Examiner's Office and the DNA Doe Project began exhuming and DNA-testing unidentified victims in 2024. No identifications had been announced as of this page's research. All named suspects and investigators in this case are long deceased.

This page lists the sources behind the claims made in this episode, tiered by how well-established each one is. Confirmed facts come from police/court records, contemporaneous reporting, or multiple independent sources. Disputed items have conflicting accounts or an unresolved question. Single-source claims rest on one account with no corroboration — treat them accordingly.

Confirmed Facts

8 items
Confirmed
At least 12 official victims are attributed to the Kingsbury Run killer (some sources count 13, including the 1934 "Lady of the Lake"), found in and around Kingsbury Run, Cleveland, between September 1934 and August 1938. Most were decapitated; many were further dismembered; male victims were frequently castrated.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer; Case Western Reserve — Encyclopedia of Cleveland History; Cleveland Police Museum
Confirmed
Only two victims were ever positively identified by name — Edward Andrassy and Florence Polillo, both via fingerprints. A third, tentatively identified as Rose Wallace, was never officially confirmed.
Source: Cleveland Police Museum
Confirmed
Eliot Ness, as Cleveland's Public Safety Director, personally interviewed Dr. Francis Sweeney and ordered the August 18, 1938 raid that burned the Kingsbury Run shantytowns — 35 officers, 11 squad cars, and 63 men detained.
Source: Cleveland Police Museum; Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
Confirmed
Dr. Francis Sweeney failed two polygraph tests administered by Leonarde Keeler, who told Ness he believed Sweeney was the killer. Sweeney was a WWI field-surgery veteran with an office next to the coroner's morgue, was never charged, and was the first cousin of Ness's political opponent, Congressman Martin L. Sweeney.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
Confirmed
Frank Dolezal was arrested in 1939 and confessed to killing Florence Polillo in self-defense, then recanted, claiming coercion. He was found hanged in custody with six broken ribs sustained while in the Sheriff's custody — officially ruled suicide, with the circumstances disputed. He was posthumously exonerated of the broader Torso killings.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer; Cleveland Police Museum
Confirmed
Willie Johnson was tried, convicted, and executed in 1944 for a separate 1942 murder with a similar dismemberment pattern. He was never conclusively linked to the Kingsbury Run killings, despite Coroner Samuel Gerber's advocacy for him as a suspect.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
Confirmed
The case is officially unsolved. According to the Cleveland Police Museum, all official police records on the case have since been lost, destroyed, or removed.
Source: Cleveland Police Museum
Confirmed
As of August 2024, the Cuyahoga County Medical Examiner's Office and the DNA Doe Project began exhuming and DNA-testing unidentified victims using investigative genetic genealogy, funded by a single donor. No identifications had been announced as of this page's research (August 2026).
Source: CBS News

Disputed / Theories

1 item
Disputed
Which of three named men — if any — was the killer. This is the episode's central debate, with zero convictions against any of the three for these specific murders. Sweeney: failed two polygraphs from the era's leading examiner, had surgical training and morgue-adjacent access, and a documented history of alcoholism and psychiatric breakdown — but was never charged, and a failed polygraph isn't physical evidence. Dolezal: the only one of the three who confessed to a killing in this case — but that confession came after injuries sustained in custody and collapsed under torture allegations. Johnson: independently convicted of a nearly identical 1942 murder — but his link to Kingsbury Run rests on a coroner's advocacy and secondhand identifications, not physical evidence tying him to these specific killings.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer; Cleveland Police Museum

Single-Source Claims

4 items
Single source
That Sweeney sent decades of threatening postcards to Ness and his family, and that they stopped the year Ness died (1957). This rests on secondary, popular-history sourcing that is itself unsourced to a primary document — worth treating as an atmospheric detail rather than a hard fact.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer (itself unsourced to a primary document on this claim)
Single source
Emil Fronek's 1934 account of a doctor attempting to drug him, linked by some researchers to Sweeney. This account is flagged by researchers as weak — Fronek reportedly could not relocate the building in question when asked to verify it.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
Single source
The claim that Ness avoided prosecuting Sweeney specifically to protect his own political standing, given Sweeney's cousin was Ness's political opponent. This is an inference drawn in later popular-history accounts, not a documented admission from Ness himself.
Source: Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
Single source
The total number of people questioned by police during the investigation — figures range from roughly 5,000 to over 9,000 depending on the source, and are inconsistent between accounts. Treat as an approximate order of magnitude, not a precise count.
Source: Case Western Reserve — Encyclopedia of Cleveland History; Wikipedia — Cleveland Torso Murderer
