The Black Dahlia

Unsolved This case remains formally open — no one has ever been charged. The two suspect theories described here are circumstantial and disputed among researchers; we don't present either as settled.

This page lists the sources behind the claims made in this episode, tiered by how well-established each one is. Confirmed facts come from court records, police reports, or multiple independent contemporaneous news sources. Disputed items have conflicting accounts. Single-source claims rest on one account with no corroboration — treat them accordingly.

Confirmed Facts

13 items
Confirmed
On January 15, 1947, a mother walking with her child discovered a body in a vacant lot on South Norton Avenue in Leimert Park, Los Angeles. It was Elizabeth Short, bisected at the waist using a technique resembling a hemicorporectomy, fully drained of blood, her face cut corner-to-corner in a "Glasgow smile," with the scene itself scrubbed clean and no blood found there.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia (citing the January 16, 1947 County Coroner autopsy)
Confirmed
Elizabeth Short was born in Boston in 1924. Her father Cleo Short's car was found abandoned on a bridge in 1930 and the family assumed he'd died, but he later resurfaced alive under a new life in California — which is part of why Short moved west in 1942, to reconnect with him.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
In September 1943, Short was arrested for underage drinking at the El Paseo restaurant's bar in Santa Barbara. The resulting booking photo became the iconic public image later associated with the case, and her arrest fingerprints are what allowed the FBI to identify her body in 1947.
Source: Santa Barbara Independent — "The Black Dahlia Never Dies"; Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
Short had no film or stage credits and worked as a waitress. The "aspiring actress" label commonly attached to her is a media invention — press coverage at the time contradictorily characterized her as an actress, a sex worker, and other labels within the same news cycle.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
Around January 24, 1947, a manila envelope arrived at the Los Angeles Examiner containing Short's birth certificate, business cards, photographs, and an address book embossed with the name "Mark Hansen." The contents had been wiped down with gasoline in an apparent attempt to destroy fingerprints, though partial prints were recovered — later compromised in transit to the FBI.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia (citing Gilmore, 2006)
Confirmed
Further taunting letters arrived on January 26 and 29, 1947, including a failed attempt to arrange a "surrender" and a note claiming the killing "was justified." An apparent suicide note referencing the killing was found in Venice, California, on March 14, 1947.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
LAPD received roughly 60 confessions during the initial investigation — a number that grew to over 500 across the following decades, some from people not even alive in 1947 — and interviewed more than 150 men as possible suspects, with roughly 750 investigators from LAPD and other departments involved at the case's peak.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
Robert "Red" Manley, a traveling salesman, was the last person confirmed to have seen Short alive. He was investigated, passed multiple polygraph examinations, and was cleared.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia
Confirmed
Dr. George Hodel, a Los Angeles physician, was tried in 1949 for allegedly molesting his 14-year-old daughter Tamar and was acquitted. Between February 15 and March 27, 1950, the LA County DA's office bugged his home; he was recorded saying, in essence, that even if he'd killed the Black Dahlia, no one could prove it, and that his secretary — who might have talked — was already dead. That wiretap file was rediscovered in the DA's archives in 2003.
Source: Wikipedia — George Hodel
Confirmed
Steve Hodel, George's son and a retired LAPD homicide detective, spent decades building the case against his father and located an LAPD file naming him as a suspect in the DA's own vault. Independent assessment of that case — including from historian William J. Mann and LAPD Detective Brian Carr — characterizes it as circumstantial: LAPD was never able to establish that Hodel and Short ever met, and Mann has suggested the wiretap statement may have been Hodel taunting investigators he knew were listening, rather than confessing.
Source: Wikipedia — George Hodel
Confirmed
A separate theory, advanced by journalist Piu Eatwell in her 2017 book Black Dahlia, Red Rose, holds that bellhop and former mortician's assistant Leslie Dillon carried out the killing on behalf of nightclub owner Mark Hansen — who had pursued and been rejected by Short, and whose address book was among the items mailed to the Examiner — with the crime scene identified as a room at the Aster Motel, where the owner reportedly found the room covered in blood the day after the murder and cleaned it up rather than report it. LAPD investigated both Hodel and Dillon seriously at the time — including tapping Hodel's phone and bringing Dillon in for questioning — without ever bringing charges against either.
Source: History.com — "Who Killed the Black Dahlia?"
Confirmed
Degnan Boulevard, a real street in Leimert Park, runs near South Norton Avenue — less than a mile from where Short's body was found. The name is a genuine geographic coincidence with Suzanne Degnan, the six-year-old victim of Chicago's unrelated 1946 "Lipstick Murder" case, in a different city.
Source: blackdahliamystery.com — "Knowledge of Leimert Park"
Confirmed
1940s-era LAPD corruption and evidence-handling problems are documented, including a September 1949 grand jury investigation into the department's homicide unit. Eatwell's book alleges that a lead detective on the case, Sgt. Finis Brown, had ties to Mark Hansen and was corrupt — a specific claim disputed by at least one other researcher (Larry Harnisch, who says he found no coverup in the DA files he reviewed). The broader claim that LAPD had a documented corruption problem in this era is well supported; the specific "corrupt lead detective" claim is contested among researchers.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia

Disputed / Theories

3 items
Disputed
George Hodel vs. Leslie Dillon/Mark Hansen vs. no named suspect. This is the case's central, genuinely unresolved debate: two well-researched, mutually exclusive theories, each backed by a different author's independent research, neither ever resulting in charges. No source treats either as settled.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia suspects
Disputed
Whether the George Hodel wiretap is meaningful evidence or overheard bravado. Independent historians are split — Mann reads it as Hodel taunting police he knew were listening, while Steve Hodel treats it as confession-adjacent. Both the wider literature and this episode present it as a live, two-sided question rather than settled fact.
Source: Wikipedia — George Hodel
Disputed
Whether 1940s LAPD corruption meaningfully compromised this specific investigation, or was a generic era-appropriate problem with no case-specific bearing. Eatwell and Harnisch disagree on this point; treating it as "a reason to distrust the whole process equally" rather than evidence for any one suspect is the more cautious, source-consistent position.
Source: Wikipedia — Black Dahlia

Single-Source Claims

2 items
Single source
The precise timing of the Aster Motel room's discovery: the best secondary source found describes the bloody room as discovered "the day following" the murder, not "the same morning" as sometimes repeated. Treat "the day after" as the more accurate framing if this detail is quoted.
Source: History.com — "Who Killed the Black Dahlia?" (summarizing Eatwell)
Single source
The idea that the Degnan Boulevard name coincidence is meaningfully significant, rather than simple geography, comes from a small number of fringe theorists rather than mainstream case literature — the more accurate framing is that it's a real but coincidental geographic overlap with an unrelated case.
Source: blackdahliamystery.com — "Knowledge of Leimert Park"
