The Somerton Man

Open Case The Somerton Man's identification as Carl Webb (announced July 2022, DNA analysis completed by SA Police in December 2023) is scientifically well-supported but has not yet been confirmed by a formal finding from the South Australia Coroner's Court. We'll update this page if that changes.

This page lists the sources behind the claims made in this episode, tiered by how well-established each one is. Confirmed facts come from court records, police reports, or multiple independent contemporaneous news sources. Disputed items have conflicting accounts. Single-source claims rest on one account with no corroboration — treat them accordingly.

Confirmed Facts

15 items
Confirmed
On the morning of December 1, 1948, John Lyons and companions found a man's body reclined against the seawall at Somerton Park beach, near Adelaide, as if asleep, with a half-smoked cigarette resting on his collar.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing contemporary Adelaide The News coverage, Dec. 1948)
Confirmed
The autopsy described a man of an estimated 40–45 years, about 5'11", with grey eyes and fair-to-reddish hair greying at the temples. He had unusually developed calves and hands showing no sign of manual labor, and distinctive wedge-shaped toes suggestive of pointed footwear or dance.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (autopsy record)
Confirmed
Every label had been removed from his clothing, and his shoes were unusually clean and freshly polished. No hat, wallet, or identification of any kind was found on him.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
Toxicology testing at the time found no identifiable poison, but pathologist John Dwyer believed the death was unnatural, and the organ findings (a congested stomach, an enlarged spleen, and congestion in the brain and pharynx) were consistent with a fast-acting glycoside-type poison — later specifically theorized as digitalis or ouabain. The coroner could not determine whether any poison, if present, was self-administered or given by someone else.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (inquest record); 1994 review by Chief Justice John Harber Phillips
Confirmed
A brown suitcase checked into the Adelaide Railway Station cloakroom on November 30, 1948, was recovered on January 14, 1949. It contained clothing bearing the laundry name "T. Keane," a stenciling brush, a trouser pocket repaired with an uncommon orange waxed thread that matched a repair on the trousers the dead man was wearing, and tools including a screwdriver, sharpened scissors, and a shortened table knife. No person named T. Keane was ever matched to the case despite a nationwide search.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
A scrap of paper reading "Tamám Shud" (Persian for "ended" or "finished"), torn from the final page of a specific 1941 Whitcombe & Tombs edition of the Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, was found sewn into a concealed pocket in the man's trousers. The matching book — missing exactly that page — was later handed to police by a man given the pseudonym "Ronald Francis," who said he'd found it discarded in his unlocked car near the beach around the time of the death.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
The book's back cover bears faint pencilled indentations of five lines of capital letters — a code that has never been definitively deciphered — along with an unlisted phone number that police traced to nurse Jessica "Jo" Thomson, who lived roughly 400 meters from where the body was found.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
Jessica Thomson independently confirmed to police that she'd owned a copy of the same Rubáiyát edition during WWII and had inscribed and given a copy to Australian Army lieutenant Alf Boxall in 1945. Boxall was found alive in Sydney in July 1949 with his own copy intact — including its final page — ruling him out as the dead man.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
A 1978 Australian Department of Defence cryptographic assessment (commissioned for an ABC documentary) concluded the code was too short to analyze definitively. A 2014 computational analysis by John Rehling found the letter pattern statistically consistent with English word-initial shorthand rather than an encrypted message, and concluded the underlying text likely can't be recovered from the surviving fragment.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing the 1978 ABC documentary and Rehling's 2014 analysis)
Confirmed
The unidentified man was buried in 1949 at West Terrace Cemetery in Adelaide, with the cost covered by a local bookmakers' association so he wouldn't be given an unmarked pauper's grave.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Confirmed
The body was exhumed on May 19, 2021 (Operation Persevere/Persist) for DNA testing, after South Australia's Attorney-General approved the exhumation in 2019 — a reversal of an earlier 2011 refusal on public-interest grounds.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man; ABC News
Confirmed
On July 26, 2022, University of Adelaide professor Derek Abbott and forensic genetic genealogist Colleen Fitzpatrick publicly announced they'd identified the Somerton Man as Carl "Charles" Webb, an electrical/instrument engineer born November 16, 1905, in Footscray, Melbourne — based on DNA recovered from hair embedded in the 1949 plaster death mask and traced through GEDmatch to distant cousins in a family tree of roughly 4,000 people.
Source: CNN, July 26, 2022; Smithsonian Magazine; ABC News
Confirmed
South Australia Police's official public response, issued the day after the announcement, stated that the DNA result had not yet been verified by police or forensic authorities and that identity confirmation would ultimately rest with the Coroner.
Source: Smithsonian Magazine (citing SA Police statement); ABC News, Nov. 2022
Confirmed
Family and divorce records show Webb married Dorothy "Doff" Robertson in 1941. The marriage was troubled; Webb attempted suicide by ether overdose in March 1946, and Dorothy left him in September 1946, filing for divorce in June 1951 on grounds of desertion (granted in April 1952). His whereabouts after 1947 are undocumented until his presumed arrival in Adelaide in November 1948.
Source: ABC News, Nov. 21, 2022 (citing family photographs and the 1951 divorce file)
Confirmed
As of the most recent reporting located, South Australia Police confirmed in December 2023 that DNA analysis of the exhumed remains was complete — but no formal Coroner's finding on identity had been issued. The Webb identification remains the strongest-supported explanation but is not yet a legal or official determination.
Source: okcir.com, 2026; clutchjustice.com, July 2026 — both lower-profile outlets rather than wire services; treat this specific status claim as the best available reporting pending confirmation from a primary SA Police/Coroner's Court statement.

Disputed / Theories

7 items
Disputed
Cause and manner of death. The 1949 inquest could not determine whether poisoning occurred, or if it did, whether it was self-inflicted or administered by someone else — suicide, homicide, and an undetected natural or accidental cause were all left open. A 1994 review by Chief Justice John Harber Phillips leaned toward digitalis poisoning as most consistent with the pathology, but that's an inference from decades-old organ findings, not a contemporaneous toxicological confirmation — no poison was ever actually detected in period testing.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man; 1994 Phillips review
Disputed
Espionage theory. The death occurred amid Cold War tensions, shortly before ASIO's founding and amid a real crackdown on Soviet intelligence activity in Australia. Proponents cite the undeciphered code, the man's unidentifiable origin, possible interest in nearby defense facilities, and a 1949 statement from a police superintendent that he believed the man came from a Warsaw Pact country — along with a 2013 claim from Jessica Thomson's daughter that her mother said the dead man's identity was "known to a level higher than the police force." This theory rests on circumstance and one family member's secondhand recollection; it has no documentary confirmation from any intelligence service. The 2022 Webb identification, if it holds up officially, undercuts but doesn't definitively disprove it, since nothing in Webb's documented biography points to an intelligence background.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (espionage-theory section, citing Kate Thomson's 2013 60 Minutes interview)
Disputed
What Jessica Thomson actually knew. Police recorded in 1949 that Thomson denied knowing the dead man or why he'd have her number. Her daughter Kate's 2013 account directly contradicts this, claiming Jessica later admitted she'd lied. The two accounts can't be reconciled and neither can be independently verified — Jessica died in 2007.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing the 1949 police interview and the 2013 60 Minutes interview)
Disputed
Robin Thomson's parentage and the Egan family connection. An anthropological analysis of ear shape and dental traits led researcher Derek Abbott to theorize that Jessica Thomson's son Robin may have been the biological child of Alf Boxall or the Somerton Man rather than her husband, with Robin's daughter Rachel potentially the Somerton Man's granddaughter. Subsequent DNA testing on Rachel reportedly showed a link to her presumed grandfather's side of the family instead, which complicates rather than confirms the theory — and it's effectively been superseded by the entirely separate 2022 Webb identification.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (DNA/exhumation section)
Disputed
How long the Rubáiyát sat in the finder's car. Sources disagree on whether the book had been in the car for "a week or two" before it was discovered, or was left there closer to the time of the death — which affects how long the dead man may have been in the area beforehand.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing conflicting newspaper vs. detective Gerry Feltus accounts)
Disputed
What the code means. There's no consensus on whether the five lines of capital letters on the book's back cover are a genuine cipher, word-initial shorthand notes (the leading modern hypothesis), a list of racehorses, or something else entirely. Both the 1978 government assessment and the 2014 statistical analysis concluded it likely can't be solved from the surviving fragment.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Disputed
The "H.C. Reynolds" seaman's card theory (2011). An Adelaide woman produced a WWI-era U.S. foreign seaman's ID card for an "H.C. Reynolds," which one anthropologist assessed as a strong facial and ear match. Extensive archival searches never turned up a matching service record, and some researchers argue the card actually belonged to a different, identifiable Horace Charles Reynolds known to have died in 1953 — which would rule the theory out entirely. Treated as a dead-end minority theory rather than a live contender following the 2022 identification.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man

Single-Source Claims

6 items
Single source
A man was reportedly seen on the beach steps looking down at the "sleeping" Somerton Man the evening before the body was discovered — this rests on one witness account.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Single source
A 1959 account describes two men seen together the night before the death, one apparently carrying the other. It comes from a single detective's (Don O'Doherty) later report, with no contemporaneous corroboration.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Single source
A hotel receptionist's account, given years after the fact, describes a guest checking out of the Strathmore Hotel on November 30, 1948, carrying a small case reportedly containing a "needle." No corroborating hotel records have been cited.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing receptionist Ina Harvey's account)
Single source
In a 2013 television interview, Jessica Thomson's daughter Kate said her mother had admitted to lying to police and had known the dead man's identity "at a level higher than the police force," and speculated both may have been intelligence-linked. This rests entirely on Kate's own secondhand recollection of private conversations with her late mother — it isn't corroborated by any document, recording, or third party, and it directly contradicts Jessica's own 1949 police statement. We don't present this as established fact, even though it's the origin of the case's most dramatic espionage claim.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man (citing Channel Nine 60 Minutes, Nov. 2013)
Single source
In January 2022, researcher Sophie Holsman proposed Austrian milliner Carl Josef Halban as the Somerton Man's identity. The nomination hasn't gained independent corroboration or wider acceptance, and predates the Webb identification.
Source: Wikipedia — Somerton Man
Single source
Descriptions of Carl Webb as moody, solitary, and at times violent when facing "defeat" come from a single source: his ex-wife Dorothy's 1951 divorce petition. That's a legal filing written by one party to establish grounds for divorce (desertion), not an independently corroborated psychological or biographical assessment.
Source: ABC News, Nov. 21, 2022 (citing the 1951 divorce file)
