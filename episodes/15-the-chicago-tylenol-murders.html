The Chicago Tylenol Murders

Open Case No one has ever been charged with the 1982 poisonings. James W. Lewis, the man most associated with the case, was convicted only of an unrelated extortion attempt and died in 2023 still denying any involvement in the murders. This case remains officially open.

This page lists the sources behind the claims made in this episode, tiered by how well-established each one is. Confirmed facts come from court records, police reports, or multiple independent contemporaneous news sources. Disputed items have conflicting accounts. Single-source claims rest on one account with no corroboration — treat them accordingly.

Confirmed Facts

13
Confirmed
Seven people died in the Chicago area between September 28 and October 1, 1982, after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been laced with potassium cyanide: Mary Kellerman (12), Adam Janus (27), Stanley Janus (25), Theresa Janus, Mary McFarland (31), Mary Reiner (27), and Paula Prince (35).
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; CBS Chicago
Confirmed
The tampering occurred after the capsules left the manufacturer: someone purchased bottles off store shelves, opened them, laced the capsules with cyanide, and returned them to shelves at multiple Chicago-area retailers, including Jewel, Osco, Walgreens, Dominick's, and Frank's Finer Foods.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders
Confirmed
The poisonings were uncovered when Arlington Heights public health nurse Helen Jensen noticed six capsules missing from the Janus family's bottle, and medical examiner's investigator Nick Pishos detected an almond-like odor consistent with cyanide; toxicologist Michael Schaffer's team confirmed that four of the 44 remaining capsules in that bottle carried nearly triple a fatal dose.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; WTTW Chicago Stories
Confirmed
Johnson & Johnson recalled the affected lot, then all Chicago-area Extra-Strength capsules, then issued a nationwide recall of roughly 31 million bottles on October 5, 1982, and reintroduced Tylenol in November 1982 in triple-sealed, tamper-resistant packaging.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; Chicago History Museum
Confirmed
James W. Lewis, writing under the alias "Robert Richardson," mailed a letter to Johnson & Johnson demanding $1 million to "stop the killing." The letter contained specific, technical detail about the tampering method that had not been made public. He was arrested December 13, 1982, and in June 1984 was convicted of extortion under the federal Hobbs Act — not the murders — and sentenced to 10 years.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis; NBC News
Confirmed
No DNA or fingerprint evidence has ever tied James Lewis or Roger Arnold, another early person of interest, to the tainted bottles. Both submitted to DNA testing in 2010 — Lewis voluntarily, Arnold posthumously via exhumation — and neither matched.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis; WTTW Chicago Stories
Confirmed
Court documents unsealed in 2009 show that Department of Justice investigators privately concluded Lewis was responsible for the poisonings but determined they lacked sufficient evidence to charge him.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis
Confirmed
Four years before the Tylenol letter, in 1978, Lewis was charged with murdering and dismembering Raymond West, a 72-year-old tax client whose remains were found in his own attic; the case was dismissed before trial on a procedural technicality, not resolved on the facts. Investigators have separately put Lewis's known aliases at sixteen or more over his lifetime, and his wife LeAnn's at four or more.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis; Biography.com
Confirmed
Roger Arnold, a Jewel warehouse dock worker, told a bartender he had potassium cyanide at home; when police searched his house, the powder they recovered tested as potassium carbonate, a common non-toxic compound, not cyanide. He was never charged in the Tylenol case. The following year he shot and killed an unrelated man, John Stanisha — whom he mistook for the bartender who'd reported him — and was convicted of that murder instead. He died in 2008, still never tied to the poisonings.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; WTTW Chicago Stories
Confirmed
Lewis was never charged with, tried for, or convicted of the murders, and denied any involvement in them until his death.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis; NBC News
Confirmed
James Lewis died on July 9, 2023, at his home in Cambridge, Massachusetts; the Massachusetts Chief Medical Examiner's office attributed his death to combined heart and liver disease, with no foul play suspected. He died shortly before appearing, via previously recorded interviews, in a Netflix docuseries that reaches no definitive conclusion on who committed the murders.
Source: GBH News; NBC News
Confirmed
The case directly led to the federal criminalization of product tampering and to the packaging industry's permanent shift to tamper-evident seals, and eventually away from loose-fill capsules toward caplets.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; Chicago History Museum
Confirmed
In 1991, Johnson & Johnson settled all outstanding lawsuits from the seven victims' families for an undisclosed sum, on the eve of trial.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; Corboy & Demetrio case summary

Disputed / Theories

4
Disputed
Was James Lewis the killer? This is the case's central, still-unresolved dispute. Federal investigators internally concluded he was responsible, and his extortion letter showed detailed, unpublicized knowledge of the tampering method. Against that: no physical evidence has ever placed him in Chicago during the poisonings or connected him to the bottles, and his own explanation for the letter shifted repeatedly across four decades without ever including a confession.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis; WTTW Chicago Stories
Disputed
Was Roger Arnold a viable alternate suspect? He told a bartender he possessed cyanide and had tangential connections to a victim's family and to the store where Paula Prince bought her bottle — but the powder found in his home was not cyanide, he was never charged in the Tylenol case, and his 2010 posthumous DNA test did not match evidence from the bottles. Most sourcing treats him as a discredited early lead rather than a live rival theory to Lewis.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; WTTW Chicago Stories
Disputed
The exact timing of Theresa Janus's death. Some accounts place her death, alongside her husband Stanley's, on September 29, 1982; a History.com account states she died the following day, September 30, after taking capsules from the same bottle a day after Stanley did.
Source: Wikipedia — Chicago Tylenol murders; History.com
Disputed
Ted Kaczynski, the "Unabomber," was asked by the FBI in 2011 to provide a DNA sample, based on the fact that his family's Lombard, Illinois address was in the same suburb as victim Mary McFarland. He declined voluntarily and denied ever having handled cyanide. Sourcing treats this as a thin, largely dismissed thread rather than a genuine competing theory, though it remains part of the documented investigative record.
Source: Wikipedia — James W. Lewis
